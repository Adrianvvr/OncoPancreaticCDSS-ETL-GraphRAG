# Targeting KRAS G12D: Advances in Inhibitor Design

ABSTRACT The KRAS G12D mutation is a highly prevalent oncogenic driver in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, colorectal cancer, and non-small cell lung cancer. Unlike other KRAS variants, G12D lacks a reactive site; therefore, it is considered “undruggable” and exhibits a propensity to activate the PI3K/AKT pathway while fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment. This review summarizes recent breakthroughs in inhibitor design that are reshaping the therapeutic landscape for KRAS G12D. Significant progress has been made in the development of small-molecule inhibitors: non-covalent inhibitors (e.g., MRTX1133) exploit ionic interactions (salt bridges) with the mutant aspartic acid residue to achieve high affinity and selectivity; novel covalent strategies are emerging, including strain-release alkylation and tri-complex inhibitors (e.g., RMC-9805). Alternative modalities such as Proteolysis Targeting Chimeras (PROTACs), peptide inhibitors, and monobodies are also discussed. The article further evaluates the status of candidate drugs currently in clinical trials and addresses the critical challenges of acquired resistance, which may arise through secondary mutations or bypass signaling pathways. Finally, it emphasizes future directions, including the optimization of drug delivery via nanoparticles and the implementation of combination therapies to enhance efficacy and achieve durable clinical responses.

## 1. Introduction

KRAS gene is among the most common mutated oncogenes in human cancers. It is a member of the RAS family located on the short arm of chromosome 12 and exhibits high homology with NRAS and HRAS. It is usually found in solid tumors including non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), colorectal cancer (CRC), and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC). The gene codes for a membrane-bound regulatory protein that mediates signal transduction through its GTPase activity by switching between an active GTP-bound state and an inactive GDP-bound state. Nucleotide exchange is performed by the G domains, Switch I and Switch II, whereas the hypervariable region at the C-terminus performs auxiliary functions. In the regular state, KRAS is resting in the form of GDP complex. Upon growth factor stimulation (e.g., EGF), guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs, e.g., SOS) promote the release of GDP and the binding of GTP to activate the protein, while GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs) enhance GTP hydrolysis to inactivate it. However, KRAS mutations result in its chronic activation that continually activates downstream proliferation and survival programs, thus promoting tumorigenesis.

KRAS targeted therapy has faced significant challenges due to its perceived “undruggability,” primarily stemming from the smooth and highly dynamic surface of the KRAS protein, which lacks suitable binding sites for small-molecule drugs, as well as its high cellular concentration necessitating inhibitors with exceptionally strong affinity. Nevertheless, recent research has achieved remarkable progress: the covalent suppression of the G12C mutation (such as Sotorasib and Adagrasib) targets the activity by covalently binding to cysteine residues; non-covalent and tri-complex covalent inhibitors are also being developed to overcome resistance and expand applicability. In addition, upstream (e.g., SOS1) or downstream effectors of the KRAS signaling pathway inhibitors are at clinical stage. These approaches have opened up a new era in the treatment of mutant tumors by blocking the KRAS activation or transducing the signals.

The G12C and G12D KRAS mutations drive cancer through divergent molecular pathways, leading to distinct clinical presentations. The G12C mutation is usually observed in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), retaining some GTPase activity and largely dependent on the MAPK pathway. Covalent inhibitors which bind the protein in an inactive form through cysteine binding such as Sotorasib and Adagrasib can effectively target it. By contrast, G12D, which occurs more frequently in pancreatic cancer and colorectal cancer, exhibits even reduced GTPase activity and a comparative predisposition to activate the PI3K/AKT pathway rather than MAPK/ERK pathway in KRAS G12C mutant in some models (e.g., NSCLC and PDAC). The aspartic acid residue interferes with hydrolysis and stabilizes effector protein binding, developing an immunosuppressive microenvironment and contributing to drug resistance. The lack of a cysteine residue at position 12, which enables traditional covalent inhibition in G12C mutants, thus necessitates alternative strategies such as non-covalent inhibitors (e.g., MRTX1133, which utilizes ionic bonding) or tri-complex covalent inhibitors targeting the GTP-bound state (e.g., RMC-9805). These differences provide critical insights for precision targeted therapy, making G12D drug development a current research hotspot.

In recent years, many advances have been made in the treatment of KRAS G12C mutations, and selective covalent inhibitors such as Sotorasib and Adagrasib, capable of binding irreversibly to cysteine, have been approved in the treatment of NSCLC. The clinical trials have shown better objective response rates (ORR) and disease control rates (DCR), with synergistic effects observed when combined with anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody. However, conventional covalent approaches based on cysteine ligand binding cannot be used in the G12D mutation since it lacks such a residue, though some newer covalent inhibitors, including tri-complex modifications, like RMC-9805 that binds the Asp-12 in the GTP-bound form, are being developed. Additionally, G12D's preference for the PI3K/AKT pathway in some models (e.g., NSCLC and PDAC) and its connection with the immunosuppressive microenvironment require the development of new strategies specific to its properties. Because the G12D mutation does not have a site direct to covalently bind, non-covalent drugs such as MRTX1133 have been created, binding to KRAS-G12D via ionic interactions with the mutant aspartic acid. Considering that G12C inhibitors primarily target the GDP-bound “OFF” state of KRAS, G12D inhibitor design must account for binding to the “ON” state or both “ON” and “OFF” states, as exemplified by compounds like BI-KRAS multi (a preclinical G12D-selective non-covalent inhibitor from Boehringer Ingelheim), TH-Z835, and RMC-9805. Another possible solution is proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs), where ASP3082 degrades KRAS-G12D by directly binding to the KRAS G12D-mutated protein and the E3 protein to form a ternary complex.

## 2. Advanced Molecular Mechanisms and Targeting Strategies for KRAS G12D

The KRAS G12D mutation drives cancer development through multiple molecular mechanisms, including protein structure, GTPase activity, and regulation of downstream signaling pathways. The G12D mutation impairs the intrinsic GTPase activity of the KRAS protein as well as GTP hydrolysis catalyzed by GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs), locking it in a persistently GTP-bound active state. In comparison with mutations such as G12C, it causes a change in the level of GTP hydrolysis without changing it enough to cause deactivation. Structurally, the G12D mutation introduces a negatively charged aspartic acid side chain that occupies the active site, disrupting the connection between the Switch I region and nucleotide-sensing residues. This anchors the KRAS docking to effector proteins and blocks the GAP-mediated control.

KRAS G12D mutation is likely to increase immune response as well as survival time by controlling tumor microenvironment, but its unique regulatory ability in the tumor environment may also be influenced by other gene mutations. For example, the loss of CDKN2A/B or abnormalities in the MET gene can make immunotherapy less effective, promote cancer growth and the accumulation of immunosuppressive immune cells, which cause a complex reaction to immunotherapy. G12D promotes the formation of KRAS dimers and higher-order oligomers (nanoclusters) on the cell membrane through a phosphatidylserine dependent interface, thereby amplifying MAPK signaling and cell proliferation. Aggregation plays a vital role in targeted therapy: it enhances signal transduction and proliferation, and thus offers a chance to prevent oligomer formation or target oligomers to reduce signal activity and increase drug sensitivity.

## 3. Advances in Small Molecule Inhibitor Development for Targeting G12D

### 3.1 Classification and Design Strategies of Small Molecule Inhibitors

Small molecule inhibitors fall mostly into two categories, non-covalent and covalent. Non-covalent inhibitors interact with the target proteins through intermolecular interactions including hydrogen bonds and salt bridges, whereas covalent inhibitors covalently bind to the protein irreversibly. Covalent inhibitors have now been able to target KRAS G12C, yet their use in G12D is challenged by the weak nucleophilicity of the carboxyl group of the aspartic acid residue. However, non-covalent inhibitors maximize fit to KRAS binding pockets and establish stable hydrogen bonds and salt bridges, such as MRTX1133, which introduces a bridged piperazine group to increase binding to G12D. Although non-covalent inhibitors may have an issue of selectivity and affinity, structural optimization and rational design provide a possible solution. Promising results of covalent inhibitors in G12C have driven KRAS drug discovery, including covalent inhibitors in development against the difficult-to-drug alleles G12R (with Arg12 acylation) and G12D (with strain-release alkylation of Asp12) yet G12D's unique properties position non-covalent inhibitors as the primary focus for current G12D-targeted research.

### 3.2 Non-Covalent Mechanisms (e.g., Hydrogen Bonds, Salt Bridges) and Design Strategies

The non-covalent small molecule inhibitors of KRAS G12D are based on intermolecular interactions (e.g., H-bonds, salt bridges, Van der Waals forces) to bind KRAS without making covalent bonding. The most important design approach includes the optimization of molecular structures to match the binding pockets of KRAS to create stable interactions. As an example, MRTX1133 exploits a bridged piperazine moiety to form an ionic interaction (salt bridge) to the mutant aspartic acid (Asp 12) to achieve selective inhibition. The Switch II pocket is a target of many non-covalent inhibitors, and MRTX1133 is one of them, with favorable potency and selectivity in a diverse range of KRAS mutants, making reversible inhibition possible. Design strategies vary by KRAS state: MRTX1133 binds both the GDP-bound “OFF” state and GTP-bound “ON” state of KRAS G12D, with significantly higher affinity and inhibitory activity for the “OFF” state, while other G12D inhibitors may prioritize dual-state or “ON”-state binding. Such inhibitors prevent the binding of effector proteins by occupying certain regions (e.g., Switch II), or they cause allosteric changes to occur so that they may be regulated. Selectivity is critical, with MRTX1133 showing over 1000-fold higher affinity for KRAS G12D than wild-type KRAS.

### 3.3 Frontiers in Non Covalent Inhibitor Development

MRTX1133 was developed by optimizing the KRAS G12C inhibitor Adagrasib. Addressing the weaker nucleophilicity of Asp12's carboxyl group compared to cysteine's sulfhydryl, researchers replaced Adagrasib's electrophilic acrylamide with bridged piperazine, enhancing G12D binding via intermolecular ionic interactions. Being a powerful, selective non covalent KRAS G12D inhibitor, MRTX1133 shows more than 1000-fold selectivity toward KRAS G12D versus wild-type KRAS. In cellular and in vivo studies, it significantly inhibits KRAS G12D signaling, demonstrating notable antitumor effects in mouse models. Of the 25 KRAS G12D tumor models, 11 models exhibited more than 30% tumor shrinkage, with pronounced efficacy in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) models.

The unusual KRAS inhibitor BI2852 promotes KRAS G12D dimerization and stabilizes dimers at the β-β interface on the cell membrane. Using HADDOCK structural modeling and NMR data, researchers constructed a stable KRAS G12D β-β dimer structure with BI-2852, effectively disrupting KRAS signaling and activity.

Developed by Tsinghua University, THZ835 is a non-covalent inhibitor that forms a salt bridge with Asp12 in the Switch II pocket, selectively inhibiting KRAS signaling in G12D mutant PDAC cell lines. It shows specificity for G12D, avoiding G12C or wild-type KRAS binding. THZ835 is effective in suppressing tumor growth in the xenograft mouse models.

INCB161734 is an orally bioavailable, potent, and selective KRAS G12D inhibitor discovered by Incyte Corporation. It targets the Switch II pocket and demonstrates antitumor activity in KRAS G12D mutant models. In preclinical research, it demonstrated strong inhibition and low off-target activities. It is currently in Phase I/II clinical trials for advanced solid tumors.

QLC1101 is a selective reversible small molecule inhibitor of KRAS G12D developed by Qilu Pharmaceutical. It binds to the mutant protein, inhibiting downstream signaling.

Preclinical data indicate efficacy in advanced solid tumors. It is in Phase I trial.

GFH375 (VS-7375) is an orally bioavailable KRAS G12D inhibitor targeting both ON and OFF states, developed by Genfleet Therapeutics and Verastem. In Phase I/II trials, it showed an ORR of 52% in PDAC and 42% in NSCLC. It is advancing in trials for KRAS G12D mutant solid tumors.

AZD0022 is a potent oral KRAS G12D selective inhibitor from AstraZeneca. It drives robust pathway inhibition in preclinical models. It is in Phase I/IIa trials for KRAS G12D tumors.

LY3962673 is a highly potent, mutant-selective, non-covalent KRAS G12D inhibitor from Eli Lilly. It shows antitumor activity in KRAS G12D models. It is in Phase Ia/Ib trials for solid tumors.

AC-4001, AC-4002, and AC-4003 are a series of KRAS G12D inhibitors from Beijing DCTY Biotech. These compounds are in preclinical/early evaluation for advanced solid tumors, focusing on selectivity and efficacy in mutant models. ### 3.4 Virtual Screening and Novel Structure Discovery

The KRAS(G12D) inhibitors were identified through a variety of methods such as virtual combinatorial chemistry and compound screening, high throughput screening, and protein engineering. For example, the compound “KD-8” discovered through virtual combinatorial chemistry and compound screening can inhibit the growth of KRAS G12D mutant cells, with a Kd of approximately 33 nM for KRAS G12D binding. Researchers have also developed a KRAS(G12D) specific monobody, 12D1. Moreover, hit compound 3, which was discovered by structure-based virtual screening, has sub-nanomolar binding affinity (Kd 0.13 nM) to KRAS(G12D), and it inhibits the proliferation of pancreatic cancer cells (IC50 43.80 nM) and suppresses tumor growth in mouse models in a dose-dependent manner.

### 3.5 Description of Covalent Inhibitors Mechanisms and Design Challenges

Covalent inhibitor development for KRAS G12D aims to design molecules that form irreversible bonds with specific residues for permanent inhibition. The lack of obvious small molecule binding pockets on KRAS necessitates targeting residues like cysteine or lysine to enhance affinity and selectivity. While G12C covalent inhibitors (e.g., Sotorasib, Adagrasib) have gained approval, G12D's absence of a cysteine residue poses significant challenges.

### 3.7 Future Optimization Strategies for Small Molecule Inhibitors

The key areas of KRAS G12D inhibitors optimization are selectivity, affinity, cell permeability, and resistance to overcome. KRAS's high cellular expression required an extraordinary degree of inhibitor affinity, and this was further coupled by the adaptive nature of its structure. Nanoparticle delivery systems (e.g., liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, exosomes) improve tumor targeting and penetration, reducing off-target effects. For example, AST2169 liposome inhibits KRAS G12D function, down regulates signaling pathways, and shows efficacy in advanced solid tumors with KRAS G12D mutations. Combination therapies pairing G12D inhibitors with chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy, or inhibitors of MEK, PI3K, or CDK4/6 pathways enhance efficacy and address resistance. Targeting G12D-induced immunosuppression (e.g., reduced T-cell infiltration, increased myeloid-derived suppressor cells) with novel inhibitors aims to improve the tumor microenvironment, offering diverse solutions to development bottlenecks.

## 4. Other KRAS G12D Targeted Drug Development Approaches

### 4.1 Peptide Inhibitors: Design and Applications

Peptide inhibitors offer an alternative for KRAS G12D by mimicking binding interfaces with effector or regulatory proteins, competitively blocking signaling. Design techniques are structure-based drug design (use G12D crystal structures to create high-affinity peptides), phage display (e.g., screen T7 libraries for KRpep-2d), and combinatorial chemistry. KRpep-2d exhibits selective inhibition, with its derivative KS-58 showing in vivo antitumor activity as a G12D-specific lead. The drawbacks such as degradation, low permeability, and short half-life are overcome by N-terminal acetylation, C-terminal amidation, D-amino acid replacement, or cyclization as exemplified by the orally bioavailable macrocyclic peptide LUNA18, which inhibits RAS-GEF interactions with nanomolar potency and has entered Phase 1 clinical trials paired with nanoparticle or antibody delivery systems to enhance stability and targeting.

### 4.2 PROTAC Technology: Targeting G12D Protein Degradation

Proteolysis targeting chimeras (PROTACs) use bifunctional molecules, one end binding KRAS G12D and the other recruiting an E3 ubiquitin ligase to degrade the target via the proteasome. Unlike traditional inhibitors, PROTACs eliminate protein, countering resistance from mutations or amplification with low-dose efficiency. Challenges include designing molecules with high affinity and E3 recruitment, overcoming permeability issues due to large size, and improving selectivity and stability. While G12C PROTACs (e.g., LC-2) have progressed, G12D specific degraders require further optimization. For instance, ASP3082 is a novel selective protein degrader targeting KRAS G12D via the ubiquitin proteasome system, showing promise in Phase 1 trials for advanced solid tumors with KRAS G12D mutations.

### 4.3 Monobody Inhibitors

Monobodies, synthetic binding proteins, provide a selective intracellular strategy for KRAS G12D. Clone 12D1, identified via phage and yeast display, selectively binds KRAS(G12D)·GTPγS, inhibiting RAF RBD binding and G12D-mediated signaling without cross-reacting with HRAS or NRAS G12D mutants. Deep mutational scanning highlights its selectivity from Switch II pocket conformations and nucleotide state differences. Delivery via viruses, vectors, or mRNA, and fusion with E3 ligases for degradation, showcase monobody potential for G12D therapy.

### 4.4 Combination Therapy Strategies: Potential to Enhance Efficacy

Due to the limited efficacy of monotherapies in treating KRAS G12D mutant tumors, combination therapy has emerged as a pivotal strategy to improve outcomes. Its core principle leverages the synergistic effects of different drugs or modalities to overcome resistance and enhance antitumor activity. Key approaches include combining KRAS G12D inhibitors with MEK or SHP2 inhibitors to block critical signaling nodes, thereby amplifying anticancer effects; pairing with chemotherapy regimens like FOLFIRI, as demonstrated in the NCT05737706 trial. Additionally, combining KRAS-targeted PROTAC degraders with other therapies is considered a novel avenue to address resistance. For example, HRS-4642 combined with Adebrelimab (SHR-A1921) shows promise in advanced solid tumors, enhancing immune responses. These strategies aim to attack tumor cells from multiple angles, though the potential increase in toxicity from combinations requires careful evaluation in future studies. Additionally, indirect targeting of KRAS signaling through upstream regulators, such as the SOS1 inhibitor BI-3406, has shown promise in limiting tumor growth driven by KRAS G12 and G13 variants by disrupting nucleotide exchange, offering a complementary approach to direct G12D inhibitors in combination regimens.

### 4.5 Overcoming Resistance: A Key Challenge for the Future

Resistance to KRAS targeted drugs involves complex, multifaceted mechanisms, posing significant hurdles to effective therapy. Tumor cells develop resistance to inhibitors through various pathways, including secondary KRAS mutations, KRAS gene amplification, and activation of bypass signaling pathways such as PI3K, as well as persistent activity of downstream

MAPK or PI3K/AKT pathways sustaining cell proliferation. Furthermore, changes in the tumor microenvironment such as cytokine or growth factor effects, immunosuppression, or angiogenesis may also contribute to resistance development. To address these challenges, researchers propose multidimensional strategies: developing novel inhibitors targeting resistant mutations, particularly for KRAS G12D; implementing combination therapies that pair KRAS inhibitors with other treatments (e.g., chemotherapy or targeted drugs) to block resistance pathways; employing sequential therapy, using different drugs in phases to delay resistance onset; and designing personalized regimens based on patient genomic and tumor profiles. Continuous innovation is essential to achieve more effective and safer treatments, ultimately breaking through resistance barriers and improving therapeutic outcomes.

## 5. Summary

KRAS mutations are among the most prevalent oncogenic drivers in human cancers. While the recent success of covalent G12C inhibitors has shattered the long-standing “undruggable” paradigm, targeting the KRAS G12D mutation presents distinct challenges. Its unique features, including the absence of a targetable cysteine residue, a propensity to activate the PI3K/AKT pathway, and the fostering of an immunosuppressive microenvironment, render G12C-directed strategies ineffective. Consequently, research has expanded to include novel modalities such as non-covalent inhibitors, covalent tri-complex compounds, PROTAC degraders, and peptide inhibitors. Although several G12D-targeted agents have entered clinical trials, none have yet been approved. The future of G12D targeting will likely depend on overcoming mechanisms of resistance and designing effective combination therapies to achieve durable clinical responses.
